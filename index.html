What you are looking at

Speech is modelled as a source passed through a filter. The vocal folds chop the airflow from the lungs into pulses (the source). The tube from the folds to the lips reshapes that buzz (the filter, a slowly varying linear system). This page estimates both from your recording and animates them.

From the audio

F0 is tracked by normalised cross-correlation every 10 ms and decides whether a moment is voiced. The tract filter is a 16-pole linear prediction fit on 25 ms frames at 16 kHz. Its peaks are the formants F1 to F3. The reflection coefficients of that fit give the areas of a 16-section lossless tube, 1.09 cm per section (Wakita's inverse-filter method). That area function drives the shape of the 3D tract and the tube model plot.

The tongue

The tongue is drawn as a solid body whose upper surface is the floor of the airway. Its shape is not filmed or measured directly: it is whatever makes the airway match the 16 tube areas estimated from the sound, pressed against a fixed palate and back wall. Where the tube is narrow the tongue rises toward the roof, and the NARROWEST readout names that spot. It shows the height and front-back position of the tongue fairly for clear vowels. It cannot show tongue grooving, side contact or the tip curling, and it is unreliable for fricatives and stops.

Nasal sounds

For m, n and ng the mouth is blocked, the soft palate (velum) drops and the sound leaves through the nose. A small neural network looks for these in every 5 ms frame. It reads the spectrum in 14 bands, compares it with the recording's own average (so the microphone matters less) and with the sound 20 to 80 ms before and after. When it fires, the velum flap swings down, the nasal passage opens and lights up, the airflow leaves through the nostrils and the mouth section of the tube turns grey.

The network was fitted on 160 labelled English sentences from 16 speakers (the public TIMIT sample), with added noise, filtering, echo and speed changes. On speakers it had not heard it finds about 9 in 10 nasals that last 50 ms or more and about 2 in 3 of the very short ones, and roughly 1 flagged stretch in 6 is not a nasal (usually the hum inside b, d, g, or a w or v). Noise, echo and background voices make it worse. The mouth closure is then drawn at the network's best guess of where it is: lips for m, tongue tip for n, tongue back for ng. That guess is right only about 7 times in 10 (ng is the weakest), and the readout shows how sure it is.

Modelled, not measured

A microphone cannot see the vocal folds. Their motion here is a two-margin model: it runs at the measured F0 and loudness, with the lower edge leading the upper edge as in a real mucosal wave, slowed like a stroboscope so you can follow it. The travelling glow and the particles show the direction of flow. They are not a pressure simulation.

How far to trust the shape

Recovering a tract shape from sound alone has many solutions. The estimate is exact for the built-in demo, which is synthesised from known tubes, and it is approximate for real voices: nasal sounds, fricatives, noise and room echo all break the model. Treat the 3D shape as a teaching picture and the spectrum, F0 and formant tracks as the measurements.
